topic: Tefilat HaDerekh — the traveler's prayer, prompted by Jacob's setting out from Beer Sheva (Gen. 28:10) and his vow that God guard him "on this way" (Gen. 28:20): when the prayer is said, from what point on the road, its plural formulation, and the Western Sephardic usage recorded in Keter Shem Tob — a fitting teaching for a fortnight in which the congregation prays at home and on the road rather than in the synagogue
topic: "Surely the Lord is in this place, and I did not know it" (Gen. 28:16) — God found in an unmarked wayside place rather than in a sanctuary; why the Holy One is called HaMakom, "the Place"; and what it means that Jacob's awareness, not God's presence, was what had been absent — a word for two weeks without a minyan

## Halakha

<p>Jacob leaves Beer Sheba and asks that the Holy One guard him on the way he is going. The Rambam gives the traveler words for the whole of it: a request on leaving a town, thanks on arriving safely, and on the road the plea to be led in peace, to have one's steps directed in peace, to be kept from enemy and ambush.<sup>1</sup> The Talmud sets the shape of the prayer. One says it in the plural, joining oneself to the community even when alone on the road. One says it after setting out, for a journey of a parasang or more. Rav Hisda stood to say it; Rav Sheshet held that walking or sitting will do, and stood anyway.<sup>2</sup></p><p>The London and Amsterdam prayer books keep the plural throughout — <em>shetolikhenu leshalom</em>, that You lead us in peace — while the siddurim of the Land of Israel and much of the Sephardic East say it in the singular, as the text of the Gemara itself does; the reason given for our plural is that a person should never set himself apart from the community.<sup>3</sup> Abudraham adds a line the printed Talmud lacks: return us to our house in peace.<sup>4</sup></p>

## Aggada

<p>Jacob stops for the night at a place with no name, because the sun has set and he has to sleep somewhere. He wakes and says that the Eternal was in this place and he did not know it.<sup>1</sup> Rashi hears regret in the words: had he known, he would not have lain down to sleep there.<sup>2</sup> Radak hears a conclusion drawn afterward — the dream taught him that this site was one where revelation comes.<sup>3</sup> In neither reading does God arrive. What changed was Jacob's knowing. A patch of ground with no altar and no name had been the house of God all along, and he learned it only when he woke.</p>
