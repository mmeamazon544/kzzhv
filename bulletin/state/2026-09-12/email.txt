TORAH READING & HAFTARAH
Rosh Hashana, first day (on Shabbat), Genesis 21:1–34; maftir Numbers 29:1–6.
Haftarah: I Samuel 1:1–2:10. Ashkenazim and Sephardim read the same passage.
Rosh Hashana, second day, Genesis 22:1–24; maftir Numbers 29:1–6.
Haftarah: Jeremiah 31:2–20. Ashkenazim and Sephardim read the same passage.
The Eternal takes note of Sarah, and at the appointed time she bears a son. Abraham names him Isaac, circumcises him on the eighth day, and makes a feast when the child is weaned. Sarah laughs, and says that everyone who hears will laugh with her. Then comes the hard part of the story: Hagar and Ishmael are sent away into the wilderness of Beer-sheba, the water gives out, and God hears the boy crying and opens Hagar's eyes to a well. The reading closes with Abraham and Abimelech swearing a pact at Beer-sheba. The maftir gives the day's offerings and calls it a day of sounding.
Hannah goes up year after year to Shiloh and comes home without a child, taunted by Peninnah and loved by Elkanah, who cannot console her. At the sanctuary she prays in her heart; her lips move and nothing is heard, and Eli the priest takes her for a drunken woman until she tells him she has been pouring out her soul. He sends her away in peace. She bears Samuel, and when he is weaned she brings him to Shiloh and lends him to the Eternal for life. Her song follows: the bows of the mighty broken, the hungry fed, the low lifted up from the dust.

REFLECTIONS ON THE PARASHA
Halakha
The Rambam counts hearing the shofar on Rosh Hashana among the commandments of the Torah, learned from the sounding named in the reading for the day.1 And yet when the festival falls on Shabbat, as it does this year, no shofar is sounded anywhere. He states the difficulty himself. Blowing on Shabbat is forbidden only as shebut, a rabbinic restraint, and a commandment of the Torah would ordinarily set such a restraint aside. The Sages nonetheless decreed against it, lest someone carry the shofar through the public domain to a neighbor who can blow it, since everyone is obligated and not everyone is skilled.2 In the Temple it was blown on Shabbat, and Rabban Yohanan ben Zakkai extended that to any place with a court.3 We have neither. So we listen on Sunday, and the obligation belongs to every person in the room alike.Keter Shem Tob records an old practice of one long teru'ah after the service, without a tekia before or after it, ascribed to Rav Amram Gaon as a way of confounding the Satan. Rav Hai Gaon held it was never an ordinance, only what certain individuals did, and the Rambam's own prayer book does not mention it. Some Ashkenazic communities blew full sets three times at the end instead. One later reading takes that last teru'ah as accompanying the Shekhina home.4

Aggada
The Talmud teaches that Sarah, Rahel and Hannah were remembered on Rosh Hashana.1 That is why we read of Sarah on the first day and of Hannah in the haftara. Hannah prays in her heart; her lips move and no voice is heard. Rashi says Eli misread her because people in those days were not used to praying without sound.2 Radak reads the verse as the Targum does: she set her heart on the prayer.3 The day is called a day of remembrance, and the two things happen together — a woman's unheard words and the Holy One taking note. Our own prayer on this day is built on hers.
