TORAH READING & HAFTARAH
Rosh Hashana, first day (on Shabbat), Genesis 21:1–34; maftir Numbers 29:1–6.
The first day's reading opens with a promise kept. The Eternal takes note of Sarah, and Isaac is born to her and to Abraham in their old age. Sarah says that God has made laughter for her, and everyone who hears will laugh with her. The boy is circumcised at eight days and later weaned with a great feast. Hagar and Ishmael are sent into the wilderness of Beer-sheba, and when the water is gone God hears the cry of the boy and opens her eyes to a well. Abraham then makes a pact with Abimelech at Beer-sheba, plants a tamarisk, and calls there on the name of the Eternal.
Haftarah: I Samuel 1:1–2:10. Ashkenazim and Sephardim read the same passage.
Elkanah goes up each year to Shiloh with his household. Hannah has no children, and her rival taunts her until she weeps and will not eat. At the sanctuary she prays and vows that if she is granted a son she will give him to the Eternal for all his days. Her lips move and no voice is heard, and Eli takes her for a drunken woman until she explains herself; he blesses her, and she goes away and eats. Samuel is born, and when he is weaned she brings him to Shiloh. Her song praises the Eternal, who breaks the bows of the mighty, raises the poor from the dust, and gives strength to the king.
Rosh Hashana, second day, Genesis 22:1–24; maftir Numbers 29:1–6.
Haftarah: Jeremiah 31:2–20. Ashkenazim and Sephardim read the same passage.

REFLECTIONS ON THE PARASHA
Halakha
The Rambam rules that hearing the shofar on Rosh Hashana is a commandment of the Torah, learned by the oral tradition from the sounding commanded for the yovel.1 Yet when the first day falls on Shabbat, as it does this year, the shofar is not sounded anywhere. Blowing is forbidden on Shabbat only by rabbinic ordinance, which a Torah obligation would ordinarily set aside. The Sages feared something else: that someone would take a shofar in hand and carry it through the street to a neighbor who knows how to blow.2 The Mishnah remembers a time when the shofar was sounded on Shabbat wherever a court sat.3 Musaf keeps its verses of Malkhuyot, Zikhronot and Shofarot; the horn waits until Sunday. In our congregation the obligation to hear the shofar and to sound it belongs to women and men alike.Keter Shem Tob records the custom of one great teru'a at the close of the service, which Rav Amram Gaon explained as confounding the accuser. Rav Hai Gaon held it to be no fixed practice, and said that nothing is lost if it is left out, since the obligation has already been fulfilled. Older Ashkenazic communities blew a full set after the prayer. The Rambam's own siddur and the Abudarham do not mention the blast at all. The author adds a reason of his own: the Shekhina rests among those who pray and rises when they disperse, and so we sound a teru'a, as it is said, God ascends with a teru'a.4 Communities differ here, and both usages are honorable.

Aggada
On a morning when no horn sounds, the haftarah gives us Hannah. She stands at Shiloh and prays in her heart; her lips move and her voice is not heard.1 Rashi says the people of that time had no practice of praying without sound, which is why Eli took her for a drunken woman.2 She was the first, and she was misread. The Talmud teaches that Sarah, Rachel and Hannah were remembered on Rosh Hashana.3 She asked for a child, and she was answered, and no one standing beside her heard a word of it. The day when the shofar rests is still a day of being remembered.
