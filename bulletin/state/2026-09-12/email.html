Torah Reading & Haftarah
Rosh Hashana, first day (on Shabbat), Genesis 21:1–34; maftir Numbers 29:1–6.
Haftarah: I Samuel 1:1–2:10. Ashkenazim and Sephardim read the same passage.
Rosh Hashana, second day, Genesis 22:1–24; maftir Numbers 29:1–6.
Haftarah: Jeremiah 31:2–20. Ashkenazim and Sephardim read the same passage.
On the first day we read of Sarah. The Eternal took note of her as promised, and at the set time she bore a son. Abraham circumcises Isaac at eight days and makes a great feast at his weaning. Sarah asks that Hagar and Ishmael be sent away, and in the wilderness of Beer-sheba God hears the cry of the boy, opens Hagar's eyes to a well, and promises that he too will become a great nation. Abraham then makes a pact with Abimelech at Beer-sheba. The maftir from Numbers names the day one of sounding the shofar.
Hannah goes up year after year to Shiloh with Elkanah, taunted for her childlessness and unable to eat. She prays without a sound, and Eli, watching her lips, mistakes her for a drunken woman; she answers that she has been pouring out her heart. Eli blesses her, she goes home, and Samuel is born. When he is weaned she brings him to Shiloh and lends him to the service of the Eternal. Her song follows: the bows of the mighty are broken, the hungry are fed, the poor are raised from the dust and seated among nobles.

Reflections on Rosh Hashana
Halakha

The Rambam rules that the mitzvah of the day is to hear the shofar, learned from the Torah's words, a day of sounding shall it be for you.1 When the first day falls on Shabbat, as it does this year, no shofar is sounded anywhere. The blowing itself is forbidden only as shebut, a rabbinic restriction, and the Rambam says a commandment of the Torah would ordinarily override it. The Sages nonetheless decreed otherwise, lest someone carry the shofar through the street to a skilled blower. All are obligated, and not all are skilled.2 Among us that all is read plainly: women and men alike hear the shofar and sound it. So on Sunday morning we hear the blasts — thirty after the Torah is returned, the blower saying the blessing to hear the shofar and shehehiyanu, then further blasts within Musaf at Malkhuyot, Zikhronot and Shofarot.3

Keter Shem Tob gathers the customs for the long teru'a after Musaf. Rav Sa'adia Gaon gave the reason as confounding the accuser; Rav Hai Gaon said it was no custom at all, only that individuals who had not heard would blow once the service ended. Mahzor Vitry has a bare teru'a, without tekia or shebarim; in Vienna they sounded full sets three times over. The Rambam's own order of prayer does not mention it. Keter Shem Tob offers its own reason: the Shekhina rests among those who pray, and as they part we sound one teru'a — God has ascended with a teru'a.4

Aggada

Hannah stands at Shiloh and prays, and the verse says she was speaking upon her heart; her lips moved, her voice was not heard. Rashi explains that Eli took her for drunk because people were not accustomed to praying without sound.1 Radak, following the Targum, reads it differently: she gathered her heart into the prayer.2 The midrash teaches that Sarah, Rachel and Hannah were remembered on Rosh Hashana.3 That is why we read of Sarah in the morning and Hannah after her. Zikkaron is the day's own word, and it begins with prayer nobody else could hear.
