TORAH READING & HAFTARAH
Vayetze, Genesis 28:10–32:3.
Jacob leaves Beer Sheba for Haran. He sleeps on the open ground at a place he cannot name, dreams of a stairway with angels going up and down, and hears the promise once given to Abraham and Isaac spoken to him. He wakes, sets up the stone, calls the place Bethel, and makes a vow. At the well he meets Rachel. Laban gives him Leah first and Rachel after, and twenty years follow: children born to Leah, Rachel, Bilhah and Zilpah, flocks multiplied under a wage that keeps changing. Jacob leaves with his household, Laban pursues, and the two make a pact at Gal-ed.

Haftarah: Hosea 11:7–12:12, the Sephardic reading (Ashkenazim read Hosea 12:13–14:10).
Hosea speaks for the Holy One in the voice of a parent: I loved Israel as a child, I taught Ephraim to walk and took them up in my arms, and they did not know I was healing them. Israel has gone after Baalim and bargained with Assyria and Egypt, and the prophet names the cost. Then the voice turns — how can I give you up, my compassion is stirred, I will not act on my anger, for I am God and not a man. The prophet recalls Jacob at the heel, Jacob wrestling, Jacob fleeing to Aram and serving for a wife, and calls Israel home to kindness and justice.

REFLECTIONS ON THE PARASHA
Halakha
Jacob leaves Beer Sheba and asks that the Holy One guard him on the way he is going. The Rambam gives the traveler words for the whole of it: a request on leaving a town, thanks on arriving safely, and on the road the plea to be led in peace, to have one's steps directed in peace, to be kept from enemy and ambush.1 The Talmud sets the shape of the prayer. One says it in the plural, joining oneself to the community even when alone on the road. One says it after setting out, for a journey of a parasang or more. Rav Hisda stood to say it; Rav Sheshet held that walking or sitting will do, and stood anyway.2The London and Amsterdam prayer books keep the plural throughout — shetolikhenu leshalom, that You lead us in peace — while the siddurim of the Land of Israel and much of the Sephardic East say it in the singular, as the text of the Gemara itself does; the reason given for our plural is that a person should never set himself apart from the community.3 Abudraham adds a line the printed Talmud lacks: return us to our house in peace.4
1. Mishneh Torah, Blessings 10:25 &nbsp; 2. Berakhot 30a &nbsp; 3. Keter Shem Tob, vol. 2, p. 634 &nbsp; 4. Keter Shem Tob, vol. 2, p. 635

Aggada
Jacob stops for the night at a place with no name, because the sun has set and he has to sleep somewhere. He wakes and says that the Eternal was in this place and he did not know it.1 Rashi hears regret in the words: had he known, he would not have lain down to sleep there.2 Radak hears a conclusion drawn afterward — the dream taught him that this site was one where revelation comes.3 In neither reading does God arrive. What changed was Jacob's knowing. A patch of ground with no altar and no name had been the house of God all along, and he learned it only when he woke.
