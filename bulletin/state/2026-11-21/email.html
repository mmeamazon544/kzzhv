Torah Reading & Haftarah
Vayetze, Genesis 28:10–32:3.
Jacob leaves Beer Sheba for Haran. He sleeps on stones at a place he cannot name, dreams of a ladder with angels going up and down, and wakes to vow that the Eternal will be his God. At the well he meets Rachel, rolls away the stone, and weeps. Twenty years with Laban follow: two marriages, Bilhah and Zilpah, eleven sons and Dinah, wages changed ten times, and flocks that multiply in Jacob's favour. He flees with his household, Laban pursues, and the two men make a covenant and part. Angels meet him at Mahanaim. For more, listen to Rabbi Joseph Dweck on the Parasha, Vayetze: https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/the-parasha-with-rabbi-dweck/id1652046044
For further discussion of the Parasha, listen to Rabbi Joseph Dweck: Selfishness or Self-development?
Haftarah: Hosea 11:7–12:12, the Sephardic reading (Ashkenazim read Hosea 12:13–14:10).
Hosea speaks of the Holy One in frankly parental metaphor: a parent who loved Israel as a child, taught Ephraim to walk, lifted him by the arms, drew him with cords of love. Israel turns away to Baalim and to alliances with Assyria and Egypt. Then comes the great turning — I will not act on My wrath, for I am God and not a human being — and a promise of children returning home. The prophet recalls Jacob himself: grasping at the womb, striving with an angel, meeting God at Beth El, fleeing to Aram and serving for a wife. Return to your God; practice goodness and justice.

Reflections on the Parasha
Halakha

Travel, in the Rambam's hands, begins and ends with speech. One who enters a city asks to come in peacefully; one who leaves asks to go out in peace, and then asks to be led in peace, to have his steps directed in peace, to be supported in peace, and to be saved from enemies and ambush along the road.1 Jacob's vow as he leaves Beer Sheba asks for much the same: company on the way, bread, clothing, a safe return. The Talmud fixes the practice. Say it once you have actually set out, with at least a parasang of road ahead, standing still if you can manage it.2 The Rambam's general rule covers all of it. Ask for mercy about what is coming, and give thanks for what has already happened.3

Gaguine records that the London and Amsterdam prayer books say the prayer in the plural — lead US, direct US — while the Talmud's own wording and the Eretz Yisrael and Turkish rites keep the singular; the Shulhan Arukh prefers the plural, so that a traveler's prayer is joined to the congregation's. The Western books also ask to reach our destination in life, gladness and peace, and add three times that God's hand guard us, rescue us, help us, after the verse about angels commanded on your behalf.4

1. Mishneh Torah, Blessings 10:25   2. Berakhot 30a   3. Mishneh Torah, Blessings 10:26   4. Keter Shem Tob, vol. 2, pp. 634–635

Aggada

Jacob lies down on stones in a place the Torah does not trouble to name. Rashi reads the place as one Scripture already knows, Mount Moriah, and hears in the words "he came upon" the language of prayer.1 Then the dream: a ladder set on the ground, its top in the sky, and the traffic on it starting from below. Jacob wakes and says that the Eternal was in this place and he did not know it.2 He had slept all night on holy ground. Later Jews call the Holy One HaMakom, the Place, because the world stands in God. A person can be inside that and only find out in the morning.

1. Rashi on Genesis 28:11   2. Genesis 28:16
