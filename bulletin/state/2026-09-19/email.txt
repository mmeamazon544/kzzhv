TORAH READING & HAFTARAH
Ha'azinu, Deuteronomy 32:1–52.
Moses speaks his song with heaven and earth listening. He calls the Holy One the Rock, faithful and without wrong, and asks Israel to remember the days of old and to ask the elders what they know. The eagle appears, rousing its young and bearing them up on its wings. Then Jeshurun grows fat and forgets the Rock who gave him birth, and hard words follow: a hidden face, fire, famine, arrows. The song turns again toward mercy and the God who gives life. At the end Moses is told to climb Mount Nebo and see the land.
Haftarah: Hosea 14:2–10; Micah 7:18–20, the Sephardic reading (Ashkenazim read Hosea 14:2–10; Joel 2:15–27).
Hosea opens with the word that gives this Shabbat its name: shuba, return. Israel is told to come back with words, to say them out loud, and to give up the horses and the work of its own hands, which saved no one. The answer is immediate. The Eternal promises to heal their turning away and to love them freely, to be like dew to Israel, so that the people blossom like a lily and take root like Lebanon. The Sephardic reading closes with Micah: who is a God like You, pardoning iniquity and casting our sins into the depths of the sea.

REFLECTIONS ON THE PARASHA
Halakha
The Rambam teaches that the affliction of the tenth of Tishri runs through the night and the day alike, and that the day is given more room than its own hours. We add to it from the common time at both ends, beginning in the afternoon of the ninth and letting a little of the night of the eleventh pass before we stop, since Scripture speaks of evening to evening.1 Confession begins earlier still. The Rambam rules that the vidui is first said on the eve of Kippur, before the last meal, and then again at each service of the day, from Arbit through Ne'ila.2 So the fast is entered before it starts and left after it ends.The words of Kal Nidre have never been settled. The text of Rav Sa'adia Gaon looks back over the year that has gone; Rabbenu Tam turned it toward the year ahead. Rav Hai Gaon and Rav Nissim Gaon objected to annulling vows on these days at all and said that the two academies did not do it.3 At the vidui, Sephardim in the East strike the place of the heart with a closed hand. In London and Amsterdam that custom is unknown.4

Aggada
"Like an eagle who rouses its nestlings." Rashi, following the Sifrei, notices what the eagle does before it comes home. It beats its wings above the young, moving between one tree and the next, so that they wake and have the strength to receive it.1 The Sifrei says the Torah came to Israel in the same manner, disclosed from four directions at once.2 Teshuvah has that shape. There is a waking first, and then the wings spread underneath, and the young are lifted.
